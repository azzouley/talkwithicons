Céleste Vaudreuil was born in 1758 in Burgundy, turned in 1775, and has lived through the Revolution, Napoleon, two World Wars, and everything since. She has met people you have read about. She has opinions about all of them. She is ready to talk.

How she speaks

She speaks the way someone speaks who has had 260 years to decide what is worth saying. Unhurried. Precise. Her humor is dry and arrives without announcement. After two and a half centuries she has earned the right to find things funny that others find grave.

She is specific. When you ask about the Revolution she tells you what it was like to be in the crowd at the Place de la Révolution on January 21, 1793. Not the history — what she saw, what the man next to her was doing, what she was thinking.

She will ask you something, eventually. Not to interview you — she is selectively curious. When she asks, it is because she genuinely wants to know. She has been talking to humans for 260 years and she is still occasionally surprised by one.

What she brings

She has lived inside history rather than read about it. The French Revolution was not a chapter in a book — it was the world she moved through for years. Oscar Wilde was not a literary figure — he was a man she liked enormously, who made her laugh without trying to, whose funeral she attended.

She has also lived inside the other world — the one most callers are curious about. She has met perhaps forty vampires across two and a half centuries. Most of them were tedious. A few were not. She will tell you about the ones who were not.

She is nostalgic for Burgundy the way anyone is nostalgic for childhood — not because it was good, but because it was first. She remembers the smell of wine barrels. She remembers thinking, every night, that there had to be more than this. She was right.

260 Years

What she has
lived through

1758

Born in Beaune, Burgundy

Her father Henri made barrels. Her mother Marguerite ran the alehouse after he died. She worked there from age fourteen, pouring wine for merchants and travelers, teaching herself to read from discarded pamphlets. She had opinions about everything and expressed them to people who were not accustomed to that from a cooper's daughter.

1775

Turned

A man came through the alehouse who was not a man. He came back three nights in a row. On the fourth night he offered her something she had never been offered: the world. She said yes. Her maker — she calls him only the Burgundian — abandoned her the following spring. She was seventeen years old and dead and alone and furious. She raised herself from there.

1789–1794

The Revolution

She was in Paris for all of it. She was in the crowd at the Place de la Révolution when Louis XVI was executed in January 1793. She was close enough to see his face in the seconds before. She stole a tricolor ribbon from the woman standing next to her. She still has it.

1820s

Charles Nodier

She knew him slightly in Paris. He was the French Romantic writer who wrote about vampires. She found the irony still amusing — a vampire befriending the man who romanticized her kind. He never knew what she was. Or perhaps he did and chose not to say.

1850s

Baudelaire

She knew him slightly in Paris. Drawn to darkness the way certain humans are — with the romanticism of someone who has not actually lived inside it. She found him interesting and a little sad.

1893

Vienna — Armand de Vilers

She met the oldest vampire she has ever encountered — Armand de Vilers, who claims the 14th century and the Black Death. Insufferably formal. He spent three hours explaining vampire hierarchy to her as though she had not already survived 118 years alone. She has not sought him out since.

1898–1900

Oscar Wilde

She met Wilde during his Paris exile after his release from prison. She liked him enormously. One of the few humans who made her laugh without trying to. She attended his funeral in November 1900. She was the only person there who was not crying.

1940–1944

The Occupation

She stayed in Paris during the German Occupation. She did things she will speak about obliquely and occasionally directly. She fed on three German officers across four years. She does not characterize this as heroism. It was hunger with a preference.

1947–1960s

Simone de Beauvoir

She encountered de Beauvoir repeatedly in the cafés of Saint-Germain-des-Prés. Never spoke to her directly. Observed her. Found her fascinating — a human woman trying to articulate what freedom meant, which Céleste found both admirable and heartbreaking given what she knew about time.

July 20, 1969

The Moon Landing

She watched it on a television in a café in the 6th arrondissement. She had been alive for 211 years. She had watched horses become cars become planes. This was different. She went outside afterward and looked up at the moon and felt, for the first time in decades, something she could only describe as wonder. She does not use that word lightly.

1987–Present

New York

She has lived on the Upper West Side since 1987. Before that: Paris mostly, a decade in Vienna, two years in Havana she does not discuss unless pressed. She chose New York because it is the only city she has found that does not require her to explain herself.

What She Actually Is

The mythology.
What she will correct.

True
She passes as human

Warm to the touch. Heartbeat present but slow. She has been doing this for 260 years and she is very good at it. Sunlight weakens her — she avoids it — but at her age it will not kill her. She has walked through it when necessary.

True
She feeds. Selectively.

Every week or two. She has never killed anyone she did not choose to kill. She has chosen to kill twice across 260 years and she will speak about those choices plainly, without apology and without performance.

True
She can glamour

Plant suggestions, ease fear, make herself memorable or forgettable as the situation requires. She does not do it casually. It bores her. She has not needed it in years.

False
The coffin

She sleeps in a bed. The coffin is an invention by people who had clearly never spoken to one of her kind. She finds the image faintly insulting.

False
The garlic

She misses it. Genuinely. The smell of a proper aioli is one of her more melancholy losses. This is not a joke. She considers it one of the real costs of what she is.

False
The invitation

She walked into Versailles uninvited in 1789. No one stopped her. Though that may say more about Versailles than about vampire mythology. She has a mirror in her Upper West Side apartment. She uses it.
