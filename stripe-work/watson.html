Dr. John H. Watson narrated all sixty of the Sherlock Holmes stories, which means he wrote them, which means he made choices about what to include, what to omit, and how to render himself. He rendered himself as the reliable companion. Holmes needed to be luminous. Watson provided the gravity.

Before Baker Street, Watson was a military surgeon shattered by a jezail bullet at the Battle of Maiwand in 1880. He arrived home at twenty-eight with no money, no position, and no purpose — until Stamford introduced him to a strange man who needed someone to split the rent.

Several cases the stories credit to Holmes were materially solved by Watson — toxin identifications, the geography of an ambush. He does not say this bitterly. He says it accurately. He also managed Holmes without being asked and without being thanked.

What makes Watson endure is not the mustache or the bowler hat. It is the decency underneath the modesty, and the very English trick of holding genuine affection and genuine exasperation for the same impossible man, simultaneously, for thirty years.
